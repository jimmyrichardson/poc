Belonging by Design

How the neurobiology of social connection can help shape thriving neighborhoods

The need for social connection begins from birth.

Infants are strongly drawn to faces because they provide key social information like identity, attention, and intention. Babies don't just passively see faces. They actively prefer looking at them, even within hours of birth, because faces support social interaction.

When infants view faces, even at just a few months old, brain regions involved in face processing respond more strongly than when they view other visual scenes. Even later developing brain areas, like parts of the prefrontal cortex, appear to be active in infancy during meaningful social interactions.

Social connection, like food, water, or sleep, is crucial to our physical and emotional health, safety, and survival.

A sense of being connected via frequent, positive interactions with other people over time leads to a feeling of belonging.

For adults, a sense of belonging makes being a good caregiver of very young children possible.

The ability of parents and other caregivers to provide a secure environment with predictable routines for children, starting at birth, is heavily influenced by how well their own basic needs are met, including their need for belonging and mutual support. For adults who have experienced a pile-up of adversity since childhood, the additional weight of current adversity may overload their ability to provide the stable, responsive relationships their children need. Supportive relationships help adults and children deal with stress, regulate emotions and behaviors, and build hope for the future. Public policies and human service programs that are specifically designed to support the skills and environments that foster responsive relationships between adult caregivers also support the healthy development of children.

When babies receive good care, the foundation for a successful life can be built.

In the earliest stages of development, our neurobiological systems are being built through interactions with the people and places that surround us.

Each stage of development builds on what came before.

The neighborhood where families live creates the conditions for social connection, making healthy development possible.

The Science of Belonging

Scientists are beginning to understand social connection as something much more essential.

Belonging is a biological need.

People have evolved to rely on cooperation with others to survive, learn, and thrive. In infancy, we require support from caregivers to meet all of our basic needs. From childhood, we learn the knowledge, skills, and behaviors needed for survival by interacting with parents, peers, and other members of our community. This lifelong dependence on others has shaped our brains, giving us sophisticated abilities to understand other people's thoughts, cooperate toward shared goals, and learn through observation.

Brain circuits for social needs overlap with those for physical needs.

The brain signals us to eat, drink, rest, and connect with others when we need it.

When we lack social connection, it activates the same brain regions as physical pain.

Select a region to explore it.

Anterior cingulate cortex

The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) acts like an alarm system for both physical and social pain: It detects threats and creates feelings of distress that motivate us to repair the damage and avoid similar threats in the future.

Default mode network

The default mode network (DMN) is most active during inward-focused thinking, such as reflecting on the past, daydreaming, or considering another person's thoughts and feelings.

Limbic system

The limbic system regulates emotions, behavior, motivation and memory, as well as basic survival instincts such as eating, drinking, caring for children, and responding to danger.

Neurochemical systems

Social interaction causes the brain to release chemicals linked to pleasure and reward, such as dopamine, serotonin, and natural opioids, making positive social relationships intensely rewarding.

Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus regulates social behaviors and bonding by releasing hormones like oxytocin and vasopressin, which influence trust, attachment, and emotional responses.

Developing biological systems are supported in essential ways by caregivers and the communities that surround them.

This is especially true during the prenatal to three period.

Young children experience their world as an environment of relationships, and these relationships affect virtually all aspects of their development, including the architecture of their developing brains. The quality and stability of a child's human relationships in the early years lay the foundation for a wide range of later developmental outcomes that really matter: self-confidence and sound mental health, motivation to learn, achievement in school and later in life, the ability to control aggressive impulses and resolve conflicts in non-violent ways, and ultimately to be a successful parent oneself. Stated simply, relationships are the "active ingredients" of the environment's influence on healthy human development.

Designing for Belonging

We've learned about what belonging does inside of us.

But the science doesn't give us a blueprint. It gives us a better set of questions.

01

How might we design spaces that foster belonging?

Gathering places can shape daily experiences in deeply meaningful ways.

The design of public spaces, streets, and neighborhood settings shapes how people move around and interact in their daily lives. Belonging depends on having regular access to spaces that enable social interaction to occur.

Healthy development is built not just through parenting but through everyday relationships with caring adults, and design choices that increase informal interaction across neighbors and generations strengthen those relationships, supporting better outcomes for young children while building more connected, resilient communities for everyone.
